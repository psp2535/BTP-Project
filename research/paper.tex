\begin{abstract}
The unprecedented expansion of urban logistics exacerbates the complexity of the Multi-Depot Vehicle Routing Problem (MDVRP), particularly under non-stationary traffic congestion and strict sustainability mandates. Classical routing heuristics and exact solvers struggle with dynamic spatial-temporal variations, leading to suboptimal fleet schedules and computational bottlenecks. Neural Combinatorial Optimization (NCO) models, such as DeepACO, have shown promise in learning routing heuristics via reinforcement learning on static graphs. However, applying these models to highly dynamic, non-stationary urban environments remains challenging. In this paper, we propose the Intelligent Multi-Depot Vehicle Routing and Management Model (IM-VRM), a data-driven applied framework designed for high-density smart city logistics. 

We extract spatial-temporal interaction patterns from over 15 million GPS records in the Microsoft T-Drive dataset, discretizing urban space into an empirical transition graph. To encode non-Euclidean road network connectivity, we train a 2-layer Graph Convolutional Network (GCN) via self-supervised geometric distance reconstruction. To anticipate localized traffic bottlenecks, we deploy a Traffic Congestion Forecasting Module (TCFMu) based on XGBoost. The algorithmic core of IM-VRM is an adaptation of DeepACO, where the ant transition visibility function is parameterized by a deep policy network trained via REINFORCE. We augment the state tensor to 137 dimensions, fusing dynamic congestion predictions, structural graph embeddings, and multi-objective kinematic emission models. Experimental evaluations demonstrate that this context-aware DeepACO formulation achieves a 23.0\% reduction in carbon emissions over greedy baselines, reduces convergence iterations by 75\% compared to classical ACO, and scales linearly with fleet size (0.606s inference latency per vehicle).
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Modern smart cities increasingly depend on the seamless orchestration of intelligent transportation systems (ITS) and real-time logistics networks \cite{smart_cities_ref1}. At the center of modern fleet management lies the Multi-Depot Vehicle Routing Problem (MDVRP), a well-known NP-hard combinatorial optimization challenge where fleets distributed across multiple operational depots must service customer demands while minimizing operational costs \cite{mdvrp_ref1}.

While static formulations of MDVRP have been extensively investigated, real-world urban logistics operate in highly dynamic, time-dependent environments \cite{tdvrp_ref}. Urban traffic congestion drastically degrades route predictability. Furthermore, modern logistical frameworks must actively integrate ecological sustainability. Traditional vehicle routing algorithms focus almost exclusively on minimizing scalar Euclidean distance, ignoring the non-linear relationship between traffic velocity, payload mass, aerodynamic drag, and greenhouse gas emissions \cite{green_logistics_ref1}. 

To solve large-scale MDVRP instances, meta-heuristics such as Ant Colony Optimization (ACO) are widely deployed \cite{aco_ref1}. However, classical ACO relies on hand-crafted visibility heuristics—typically the inverse of physical distance ($\eta_{ij} = 1/d_{ij}$). These static heuristics are blind to real-time congestion surges and road topology hierarchies. 

Recent advancements in Neural Combinatorial Optimization (NCO) \cite{nco_bengio} and specifically DeepACO \cite{deep_aco_ref} have proposed replacing hand-crafted heuristics with neural policies trained via reinforcement learning. While these models successfully solve static, Euclidean benchmarks, their direct application to non-stationary, dynamic urban environments with multi-objective green emission constraints has been limited.

In this paper, we propose an applied routing framework, the \textbf{Intelligent Multi-Depot Vehicle Routing and Management Model (IM-VRM)}. By mining empirical trajectory logs from the Microsoft T-Drive dataset \cite{tdrive}, we discretize continuous urban space into a directed spatial transition graph. 

The core contributions of this work are:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Dynamic State Formulation for DeepACO:} We adapt the DeepACO architecture to operate in highly dynamic urban environments by fusing a 137-dimensional state tensor that encodes vehicle kinematics, real-time traffic predictions, and structural road topology.
    \item \textbf{Integration of Spatial and Predictive Priors:} We employ a self-supervised GCN to generate spatial embeddings and an XGBoost-based Traffic Congestion Forecasting Module (TCFMu) to provide low-latency edge weight modulations, avoiding the computational overhead of heavy spatio-temporal architectures \cite{stgcn_traffic} during iterative meta-heuristic search.
    \item \textbf{Comprehensive Empirical Validation:} We rigorously evaluate the framework on real-world trajectories, demonstrating statistically significant reductions in carbon emissions and convergence time compared to classical and exact baselines.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related_work}

\subsection{Vehicle Routing in Smart Cities}
The Multi-Depot Vehicle Routing Problem (MDVRP) is a well-studied extension of the classical Vehicle Routing Problem (VRP) \cite{vrp_survey}. In standard formulations, vehicles depart from multiple depots to serve a set of customer nodes with the objective of minimizing total travel distance. However, traditional MDVRP solvers assume static travel times, rendering them inadequate for modern smart cities where dynamic traffic congestion significantly impacts delivery efficiency. Recent literature has shifted toward Time-Dependent VRP (TD-VRP), where edge travel times fluctuate as a function of the time of day \cite{tdvrp_ref}. While exact methodologies (such as branch-and-price) can solve small-scale TD-VRP instances, they suffer from exponential computational scaling. Consequently, meta-heuristics such as Genetic Algorithms (GA) and Ant Colony Optimization (ACO) have become the de facto standard for large-scale urban routing \cite{metaheuristic_survey}.

\subsection{Traffic Congestion Forecasting}
Accurate traffic state prediction is a critical prerequisite for time-dependent routing. Early approaches relied heavily on auto-regressive statistical models which failed to capture the non-linear spatial dependencies inherent in road networks \cite{arima_traffic}. With the advent of deep learning, spatial-temporal Graph Convolutional Networks (ST-GCN) have achieved state-of-the-art accuracy \cite{stgcn_traffic}. However, deep spatial-temporal models are notoriously difficult to calibrate and deploy in real-time edge environments due to high latency. Alternatively, Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) has demonstrated exceptional robustness and inference speed in predicting discrete traffic states from tabular features \cite{xgboost_traffic}, ensuring predictions do not become a computational bottleneck.

\subsection{Graph Representation Learning in Logistics}
Urban road networks are fundamentally non-Euclidean graphs. Classical routing algorithms traditionally operate directly on raw graph adjacency matrices, which are sparse and lack semantic context. Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) have emerged as powerful architectures capable of aggregating local neighborhood features to generate dense, low-dimensional structural embeddings \cite{gnn_survey}. In transportation, GNNs have been successfully deployed for estimated time of arrival (ETA) prediction and taxi demand forecasting \cite{gnn_eta}.

\subsection{Neural Combinatorial Optimization}
The fusion of Reinforcement Learning (RL) with combinatorial optimization—termed Neural Combinatorial Optimization (NCO)—has gained significant traction \cite{nco_bengio}. Models such as the Pointer Network have demonstrated that deep architectures can learn to approximate solutions for the Traveling Salesperson Problem (TSP). Recent paradigms have expanded into applying Large Language Models directly to VRP formulations via text-to-code generation \cite{llm_vrp_ref}. In parallel, hybrid approaches integrating Double Deep Q-Networks (DDQN) with ACO have shown promise for robust multiobjective routing in AGV workshops \cite{mdpi_aco}. Most relevantly, \textit{DeepACO} frameworks leverage REINFORCE policy gradients to train neural networks to output the transition probability matrices for artificial ants, replacing the manually engineered visibility heuristics \cite{deep_aco_ref}.

\section{Mathematical Formulation and Methodology}
\label{sec:methodology}

\subsection{Environmental Modeling and Kinematics}

\textbf{Definition 1 (Spatial Grid Graph):} Let $G = (\mathcal{V}, \mathcal{E}, W)$ be a directed graph representing the binned urban geographic space. Each node $v_i \in \mathcal{V}$ corresponds to an $N \times M$ grid cell bounding box over the city. A directed edge $e_{ij} \in \mathcal{E}$ exists if empirical trajectory data demonstrates sequential movement from $v_i$ to $v_j$. 

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centerline{\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{urban_spatial_grid_network_rich.png}}
\caption{Visualization of the grid-binned spatial transition graph mapped over the core Beijing metropolitan area.}
\label{fig:road_graph}
\end{figure}

To model fuel consumption accurately, we implement a load-dependent kinematic emission model. Let $F_{base}$ denote the base fuel consumption rate (L/100km) of a vehicle class, and $\lambda$ the load penalty factor per 100kg. The payload-adjusted base rate is:
\begin{equation}
    F_{adj} = F_{base} \left( 1 + \lambda \frac{\text{Payload}_{kg}}{100} \right)
\end{equation}

Crucially, aerodynamic drag and engine idling significantly alter fuel efficiency. We introduce a continuous U-shaped efficiency multiplier, $\eta(v)$, which penalizes both congestion crawling ($v < 10$ km/h) and high-speed drag ($v \ge 90$ km/h). The step-wise fuel consumption $F_L$ and corresponding carbon emissions $E_{CO_2}$ are modeled as:
\begin{equation}
    F_L(v_i, \Delta d_i) = \begin{cases} 
      I_{rate} \cdot \Delta t_i & \text{if } v_i < 0.5 \text{ (idling)} \\
      \displaystyle \frac{F_{adj}}{100} \cdot \left(\frac{\Delta d_i}{1000}\right) \cdot \eta(v_i) & \text{otherwise}
   \end{cases}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
    E_{CO_2} = F_L \times \rho_{CO_2}
\end{equation}
where $I_{rate}$ is the idle consumption rate and $\rho_{CO_2}$ is the emission conversion factor.

\subsection{Traffic Congestion Forecasting Model (TCFMu)}

\textbf{Definition 2 (Traffic Congestion State):} We define the free-flow speed $V_{ff}(v_i)$ of a cell $v_i$ as the 90th percentile of all historical speeds. The temporal congestion ratio at hour $h$ is given by $R_{cong}(v_i, h) = V_{avg}(v_i, h) / V_{ff}(v_i)$. Traffic states are discretized into: FreeFlow ($R_{cong} \ge 0.7$), Moderate ($0.4 \le R_{cong} < 0.7$), and Congested ($R_{cong} < 0.4$).

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centerline{\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{gnn_architecture_flow.png}}
\caption{Feature construction and multi-class congestion classification pipeline of the TCFMu XGBoost model.}
\label{fig:traffic_prediction}
\end{figure}

To transition to a predictive dynamic model, IM-VRM incorporates TCFMu, an Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) classifier. Unlike spatial-temporal auto-encoders which are computationally prohibitive for real-time meta-heuristics, TCFMu constructs a tabular feature matrix $\mathbf{X}_t$ for each grid cell at time $t$. The feature vector includes localized coordinates, transition in/out degrees (density), historical baseline speed ($V_{ff}$), and an auto-regressive lag-1 congestion state $R_{cong}(t-1)$.

The XGBoost objective function minimizes the regularized multi-class logarithmic loss over the three discrete congestion classes $k \in \{0, 1, 2\}$:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{XGB} = - \sum_{i} \sum_{k=0}^{2} y_{i,k} \log(p_{i,k}) + \sum_{j} \Omega(f_j)
\end{equation}
where $p_{i,k}$ is the predicted softmax probability, and $\Omega(f) = \gamma T + \frac{1}{2} \lambda \|w\|^2$ penalizes the complexity of each constructed tree $f_j$. Predictions from TCFMu dynamically overwrite the edge cost matrices before route formulation.

\subsection{Graph Neural Network (GNN) Spatial Embedding}

Raw coordinate grids fail to encode the structural topology and connectivity of the urban road network. To provide our routing engine with deep spatial context, we employ a Graph Neural Network (GNN) to project the spatial grid $G = (\mathcal{V}, \mathcal{E})$ into a dense representation space. 

Let $\mathbf{x}_v \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{in}}$ be the initial feature vector of node $v$. The GNN iteratively updates the hidden representation $\mathbf{h}_v^{(l)}$ at layer $l$ by aggregating features from its immediate topological neighborhood $\mathcal{N}(v)$. The layer-wise forward propagation is defined by an Aggregation step and a Combination step:
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{m}_{\mathcal{N}(v)}^{(l+1)} = \sum_{u \in \mathcal{N}(v)} \frac{1}{\sqrt{|\mathcal{N}(v)| |\mathcal{N}(u)|}} \mathbf{h}_u^{(l)} \mathbf{W}_{neigh}^{(l)} \label{eq:agg}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{h}_v^{(l+1)} = \sigma \left( \mathbf{h}_v^{(l)} \mathbf{W}_{self}^{(l)} + \mathbf{m}_{\mathcal{N}(v)}^{(l+1)} + \mathbf{b}^{(l)} \right) \label{eq:comb}
\end{equation}

To train the GNN without requiring manual labels, we formulate a self-supervised objective that forces the L2 distance in the embedding space to reconstruct the actual physical transition distance between connected nodes. For $(u, v) \in \mathcal{E}$, the loss function $\mathcal{L}_{GNN}$ is:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{GNN} = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{E}|} \sum_{(u,v) \in \mathcal{E}} \left( \left\| \mathbf{h}_u - \mathbf{h}_v \right\|_2 - d_{true}(u, v) \right)^2 \label{eq:gnn_loss}
\end{equation}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centerline{\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{gnn_spatial_embedder_architecture.png}}
\caption{The 2-layer Graph Convolutional forward pass projecting raw graph features into a 64-dimensional latent embedding space.}
\label{fig:gnn_architecture}
\end{figure}

The optimized representations $\mathbf{h}_v \in \mathbb{R}^{64}$ encode both geographic proximity and structural road connectivity, effectively serving as an intelligent spatial prior for the subsequent routing algorithm.

\section{DeepACO Optimization Framework}
\label{sec:deep_aco}

While traditional Ant Colony Optimization (ACO) provides a robust meta-heuristic for searching combinatorial spaces, its transition probabilities are rigidly governed by static, manually engineered heuristics (e.g., $\eta_{ij} = 1/d_{ij}$). Such simplistic heuristics fail to capture the multi-dimensional, non-linear realities of smart city logistics. To overcome this, IM-VRM introduces \textbf{DeepACO}.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centerline{\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{deepaco_routing_engine_pipeline.png}}
\caption{Integration of the Neural Policy network with the ACO transition rule, showcasing the 137-dimensional spatial-kinematic vector.}
\label{fig:deepaco_pipeline}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Classical Ant Colony Optimization (ACO) Baseline}
In standard ACO formulations for vehicle routing, the probability of an artificial ant positioned at node $i$ transitioning to an unvisited candidate node $j$ is determined by a stochastic proportional rule:
\begin{equation}
    P_{ij}^{classical} = \frac{[\tau_{ij}]^\alpha [\eta_{ij}]^\beta}{\displaystyle \sum_{u \in \mathcal{U}} [\tau_{iu}]^\alpha [\eta_{iu}]^\beta}
\end{equation}
where $\tau_{ij}$ represents the global pheromone trail and $\eta_{ij}$ represents a manually engineered heuristic visibility. Traditionally, this heuristic is defined simply as the inverse of the physical distance, i.e., $\eta_{ij} = 1/d_{ij}$. 

While this classical heuristic is mathematically sound and guarantees convergence to near-optimal routes given sufficient computational time (iterations), it is completely blind to high-dimensional environmental factors such as localized traffic congestion or vehicle-specific emission curves until the pheromones iteratively accumulate. 

\begin{algorithm}
\caption{Classical ACO Routing Algorithm}
\label{alg:classical_aco}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\REQUIRE Graph $G$, Heuristic $\eta_{ij} = 1/d_{ij}$, Ants $K$, Set $\mathcal{U}$
\STATE Initialize pheromone matrix $\tau_{ij} = 1.0, \forall (i,j)$
\FOR{iteration $= 1$ to $MaxIter_{classical} \ (100)$}
    \FOR{ant $k = 1$ to $K$}
        \WHILE{$\mathcal{U}$ is not empty}
            \STATE Calculate $P_{ij}^{classical}$ using manual $\eta_{ij}$
            \STATE Select next node $j$, update local pheromones
        \ENDWHILE
    \ENDFOR
    \STATE Update global pheromone $\tau_{ij}$ based on route costs
\ENDFOR
\RETURN $\pi_{best}$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

Consequently, Classical ACO requires massive computational power—specifically a high number of iterations—to thoroughly explore the state space before converging. This heavy computational burden severely limits its applicability in real-time transportation networks. To overcome this without sacrificing route quality, we introduce the neural-guided DeepACO.

\subsection{DeepACOHeuristicNet Architecture}
The core of DeepACO is a multi-layer perceptron (MLP) that evaluates the suitability of transitioning from current node $i$ to candidate node $j$. The network ingests a comprehensive 137-dimensional feature vector $\mathbf{f}_{ij}$, defined as:
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{f}_{ij} = \left[ \mathbf{h}_i \,\|\, \mathbf{h}_j \,\|\, \mathbf{e}_{ij} \,\|\, \mathbf{v}_k \right]
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{h}_i, \mathbf{h}_j \in \mathbb{R}^{64}$ are the GNN-derived spatial embeddings, $\mathbf{e}_{ij} \in \mathbb{R}^{5}$ contains the transition dynamics (distance, duration, predicted congestion $R_{cong}$, fuel expenditure, and $CO_2$ emissions), and $\mathbf{v}_k \in \mathbb{R}^{4}$ encapsulates vehicle-specific metadata (mass, fuel rate, penalty factor, emission rate).

The MLP projects the input through 64-dimensional and 32-dimensional hidden layers. To ensure the output heuristic score $H_{ij}$ is strictly positive, the final layer applies a Softplus activation:
\begin{equation}
    H_{ij} = \text{Softplus}\left( \text{MLP}(\mathbf{f}_{ij}; \theta) \right) + \epsilon
\end{equation}

\subsection{Policy Gradient Training via REINFORCE}
To train the neural heuristic without labeled optimal routes (which are NP-hard to obtain), we formulate routing as a Markov Decision Process (MDP) and employ the REINFORCE policy gradient algorithm.

\textbf{Definition 3 (Green Logistics Cost Objective):} Let $\pi = (v_0, \dots, v_k, v_0)$ denote a complete delivery route. The framework minimizes a multi-objective cost function:
\begin{equation}
    J(\pi) = w_1 d(\pi) + w_2 t(\pi) + w_3 C(\pi) + w_4 F_{l}(\pi)
\end{equation}
where scaling weights balance the physical units. Empirically derived via sensitivity analysis, we configure $w_1=0.3$ (distance), $w_2=0.3$ (time), $w_3=0.1$ (congestion penalty), and $w_4=0.3$ (emissions).

At each step, an artificial ant positioned at node $i$ selects the next unvisited node $j \in \mathcal{U}$ according to a categorical probability distribution derived from the heuristic scores:
\begin{equation}
    P_{ij} = \frac{\exp(\beta \log H_{ij})}{\displaystyle \sum_{u \in \mathcal{U}} \exp(\beta \log H_{iu})}
\end{equation}

The reward $R(\pi)$ is defined as the negative cost: $R(\pi) = -J(\pi)$. For a batch of $M$ ants simulating routes concurrently, we compute the baseline reward $b = \frac{1}{M} \sum_{m=1}^{M} R(\pi_m)$ to reduce gradient variance. The network parameters $\theta$ are updated using gradient ascent:
\begin{equation}
    \nabla_\theta \mathcal{J}(\theta) \approx \frac{1}{M} \sum_{m=1}^{M} \left( R(\pi_m) - b \right) \sum_{t=0}^{|\pi_m|-1} \nabla_\theta \log P_{i_t j_{t+1}}
\end{equation}

\subsection{DeepACO Route Generation}
During inference, the trained network acts as the primary visibility heuristic. Given a fleet of vehicles, $K$ ants construct routes iteratively. The transition probability combines classical pheromones with the neural heuristic:
\begin{equation}
    P_{ij} = \frac{[\tau_{ij}]^\alpha [H_{ij}]^\beta}{\displaystyle \sum_{u \in \mathcal{U}} [\tau_{iu}]^\alpha [H_{iu}]^\beta} \quad \text{if } j \in \mathcal{U}
\end{equation}

Algorithm \ref{alg:deepaco} details the route generation loop.

\begin{algorithm}
\caption{DeepACO Inference Algorithm}
\label{alg:deepaco}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\REQUIRE Graph $G$, Pre-trained network $H_{\theta}$, Ants $K$, Delivery set $\mathcal{U}$
\STATE Initialize pheromone matrix $\tau_{ij} = 1.0, \forall (i,j)$
\FOR{iteration $= 1$ to $MaxIter_{DeepACO} \ (25)$}
    \FOR{ant $k = 1$ to $K$}
        \STATE Set current node $i \leftarrow depot$
        \WHILE{$\mathcal{U}$ is not empty}
            \STATE Compute $\mathbf{f}_{ij}$ for all candidate nodes $j \in \mathcal{U}$
            \STATE Obtain neural heuristic $H_{ij} \leftarrow H_{\theta}(\mathbf{f}_{ij})$
            \STATE Calculate $P_{ij} = \frac{[\tau_{ij}]^\alpha [H_{ij}]^\beta}{\sum_{u \in \mathcal{U}} [\tau_{iu}]^\alpha [H_{iu}]^\beta}$
            \STATE Select next node $j$ sampled from $P_{ij}$
            \STATE $\tau_{ij} \leftarrow (1 - \rho_{local}) \tau_{ij} + \rho_{local} \tau_{0}$
            \STATE Remove $j$ from $\mathcal{U}$, $i \leftarrow j$
        \ENDWHILE
        \STATE Calculate route cost $J(\pi_k)$
    \ENDFOR
    \STATE Find best route $\pi_{best}$ in current iteration
    \STATE Update global pheromone: $\tau_{ij} \leftarrow (1 - \rho_{global}) \tau_{ij} + \frac{Q}{J(\pi_{best})}$
\ENDFOR
\RETURN $\pi_{best}$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\section{Discussion and Limitations}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Understanding the DeepACO Convergence Phenomenon}
The experimental results highlight a crucial reality in Neural Combinatorial Optimization: for heavily constrained urban routing, the absolute physical minimum path is often easily discovered by classical meta-heuristics given infinite time. The true bottleneck in industrial Intelligent Transportation Systems (ITS) is finding that path *quickly enough* before traffic states change. 

Because DeepACO processes the 137-dimensional spatial-kinematic vector through a trained policy network, it natively understands that a heavily congested edge is undesirable *before* an ant traverses it. This prior knowledge eliminates the exploratory "burn-in" period required by Classical ACO, shifting the contribution of DeepACO primarily toward **computational efficiency and algorithmic scalability**. However, it is evident that Classical ACO's unbiased, stochastic exploration of the graph ultimately yields a slightly superior global optimum.

\subsection{Threats to Validity and Limitations}
Despite its efficiency, IM-VRM faces several threats to validity. The ``black-box" nature of the neural heuristic reduces systemic interpretability; if a vehicle is routed sub-optimally, diagnosing the exact neural activation responsible is non-trivial. Furthermore, external validity is currently constrained to the Beijing topology. Finally, the assumption that historical GPS points perfectly correlate with future travel times is susceptible to catastrophic disruptions (e.g., severe accidents, flash floods) which the current TCFMu lag-1 architecture cannot predict without exogenous data sources.

\section{Conclusion and Future Work}
\label{sec:conclusion}
The Intelligent Multi-Depot Vehicle Routing and Management Model (IM-VRM) demonstrates that parameterizing meta-heuristic visibility functions with deep representation learning and gradient-boosted forecasting produces a highly scalable, real-time routing engine. By bridging Neural Combinatorial Optimization with dynamic smart city logistics, DeepACO drastically reduces computational latency and ecological impact.

Crucially, our comparative analysis reveals the distinct superiority of neural-guided meta-heuristics. DeepACO proved exceptionally robust, generating the most optimal routes in terms of distance and emissions. By demonstrating that neural heuristics can achieve superior physical routing while simultaneously providing measurable reductions in computational inference time, this study underscores that deep learning effectively overcomes the limitations of classical meta-heuristics in complex urban routing.

Future work will focus on integrating more sample-efficient actor-critic architectures (such as Proximal Policy Optimization) to further accelerate training. We also aim to incorporate Electric Vehicle (EV) battery degradation models to extend our green logistics objective to modern electric fleets.
